Start here

If you only have a short time, do these things first. The rest of the guide is there when you need a particular app.

  1. On your iPhone now, keep using the official Google apps (Gmail, Calendar, Drive, Maps, YouTube, Chrome and so on) with VoiceOver. They work. The web versions at gmail.com, calendar.google.com and drive.google.com are often even stronger once you turn on each site’s keyboard shortcuts and, in Docs, Sheets and Slides, turn on screen reader support.
  2. Turn on 2-Step Verification for your Google Account, add a recovery email and phone, and store a set of backup codes somewhere safe that you can reach without that phone. See Google Account and security.
  3. When your Pixel arrives, turn it on and, on the first setup screens, hold down both volume buttons for a few seconds. TalkBack should start and a tutorial should open automatically. Take that tutorial slowly. Official steps: Turn on TalkBack.
  4. Learn four TalkBack habits that will carry you through almost every app: swipe right or left to move, double-tap to activate, two-finger swipe to scroll, and three-finger tap to open the TalkBack menu. A full comparison with VoiceOver is in Moving from VoiceOver to TalkBack.
  5. Because you prefer voice and sometimes find dictation unreliable, turn on two extra tools early: Gboard voice typing and Voice Access. Voice Access can open apps, tap buttons and type by spoken commands, which is especially helpful when you do not want to touch the screen.
  6. On the Pixel, turn on detailed voice guidance in Google Maps before you try an unfamiliar walking route, and install Lookout for reading text, describing images and finding objects around you.
  7. When you open any of your web apps in Chrome on Android, give the page one deliberate double-tap first if you want vibration feedback. The Vibration API works in Chrome on Android after a user action. It does not work in Safari on iPhone. See How web vibration works in Android Chrome.

This guide has been researched from Google’s official help pages and a small number of reputable supporting sources, current as far as those pages allowed in late November 2026. It has not been tested on an iPhone with VoiceOver, or on a Pixel with TalkBack, by the author. Where a gesture or feature could not be verified, that is stated in the section and again in What could not be verified.

About this guide and its limits

Each app section follows the same pattern: what the app is for, how to use it with TalkBack on Android, how to use it with VoiceOver on iPhone or on the web, the most useful gestures and keyboard shortcuts, and known pain points with honest workarounds.

A few rules used while writing:

  • Gestures are taken from official Google or Apple help. They are not invented. If a help page listed a shortcut but the label was missing or unclear in the text that could be retrieved, that shortcut is not listed here.
  • Features often depend on the phone model, the Android or iOS version, the country, the language, and whether a feature is still rolling out. UK availability is noted where official pages mention it. Some AI features, such as Gboard’s “Fix it”, are officially limited to the United States.
  • Pixel 10 and Pixel 11, including Pro and Fold models, are named in current official Google help, so this guide treats both as real products. Exact shop availability, prices and every UK software difference have not been independently checked.
  • Google changes menus from time to time. If a path in Settings does not match what you hear, use the Settings search box, or ask Gemini or Google Assistant: “Open TalkBack settings”.
  • Several official Google pages still mention older TalkBack “context menus”. Those were replaced years ago by the TalkBack menu and reading controls. Where a page looks dated, this guide says so.

Google Account and security

What it is for

Your Google Account is the key to Gmail, Calendar, Drive, Photos, YouTube and the rest. On a new Pixel you will sign in once, and most Google apps will then share that sign-in. Protecting the account matters more than almost any other step in this guide, because losing it would lock you out of email, files and photos.

What to set up first

  • Open myaccount.google.com and go to Security, or Security and sign-in.
  • Turn on 2-Step Verification. Official steps: Turn on 2-Step Verification.
  • Add a recovery email and a recovery phone number that you can actually reach.
  • Set up Google prompts on your current iPhone (through the Gmail, Google, Photos or YouTube app signed in to your account) and later on the Pixel. Google recommends prompts rather than text-message codes, because text messages can be attacked through the phone number.
  • Create a passkey on your phone, using the device lock (PIN, fingerprint or face). A passkey proves you have the device, so it can replace the password-plus-second-step dance. Official help: Protecting your personal info with 2-Step Verification.
  • Download or print a set of 8-digit backup codes and store them somewhere safe that is not only on the same phone. Official help: Sign in with backup codes.
  • Consider Google Authenticator or another one-time-code app as a backup method that still works without a mobile signal.
  • Never read a verification code out to someone who calls or emails claiming to be Google. Official help is clear: Google will not phone you to ask for a code, and you should never share one.

With VoiceOver on iPhone, or on the web

The Google Account site works with VoiceOver in Safari or Chrome. Headings and form fields are generally labelled. If a security page feels awkward in a Google app, open it in Safari instead. After you connect a Bluetooth keyboard, standard web navigation (VoiceOver plus Tab, or VoiceOver Quick Nav off plus the site’s own controls) is usually calmer than poking a compact mobile layout.

With TalkBack on Android

On a Pixel, Settings, then Google, or the Google app, then your profile, leads to the same account. TalkBack will speak password characters in secure fields unless you change that in TalkBack settings. If you are in a public place, you may prefer to hide the screen (TalkBack menu, Hide or show screen) while entering a password, and to stop TalkBack speaking typed password letters.

Pain points and workarounds

  • Account recovery can be slow and stressful if you lose the phone that receives prompts. Set up at least two methods before you retire the iPhone: a recovery email, backup codes, and if possible a passkey or security key on a second device.
  • A new 2-Step Verification phone number may not be fully trusted for up to seven days. Official help mentions this delay. Do not remove the old number until the new one is trusted.
  • The Advanced Protection Programme is Google’s strongest option, built around security keys or passkeys. It is excellent against phishing, but backup codes cannot be downloaded if you are in that programme, so you need a spare key. Only turn it on if you understand the recovery path.
  • On a trusted computer or phone of your own, you can tick “Don’t ask again on this device”. Do not do that on a shared or borrowed machine.

Moving from VoiceOver to TalkBack

TalkBack is the screen reader built into Android, including Pixel phones. It is part of the Android Accessibility Suite. In daily use it does the same job as VoiceOver: it speaks what has focus, and it changes the meaning of taps and swipes so that a single tap does not activate something by accident.

The good news is that the everyday gestures are closer than they used to be. Swipe right for the next item, swipe left for the previous item, and double-tap to activate are the same idea on both systems. The differences that catch people out are scrolling, the rotor, and system gestures such as Home and Back. Those are covered below, from official Google and Apple pages.

On a modern Pixel (Pixel 3 and later, including Pixel 10 and 11), TalkBack supports both one-finger and multi-finger gestures. Official help: Get started on Android with TalkBack and Use TalkBack gestures.

Turning TalkBack on at first boot

This is the most important setup gesture. Official Google help for both Android and Pixel says:

  • When you set up the phone, hold down both volume buttons for a few seconds. TalkBack turns on.
  • You can keep using the volume buttons as a shortcut to turn TalkBack on or off later, unless you disable that shortcut in Settings, Accessibility, TalkBack, TalkBack shortcut.
  • The first time TalkBack turns on, the TalkBack tutorial opens automatically. Take it. You can open it again later from the TalkBack menu, TalkBack settings, Tutorial and help, then Tutorial.

After setup, you can also:

  • Open Settings, Accessibility, TalkBack, and choose Use TalkBack.
  • Say “Hey Google, turn on TalkBack” or “Hey Google, turn off TalkBack”, once Gemini or Google Assistant is set up.
  • On Android 16 and later, with an external keyboard, press Action + Alt + T. Action is the Windows key on a Windows keyboard, or Command on a Mac keyboard. Official source: Turn on TalkBack.

If someone sighted is helping you unbox the phone, ask them not to rush past the first screens. You want TalkBack running before Wi-Fi, Google Account sign-in and fingerprint or PIN setup, so that you can hear those steps.

Core gestures, compared with VoiceOver

These are the TalkBack 9.1 and later gestures, which apply on a current Pixel. One-finger gestures work on all Android phones. Multi-finger gestures work on updated Android 11 (Android R) with TalkBack 9.1, on Pixel 3 and later, and on some other brands such as Samsung.

To check that multi-finger gestures work: with TalkBack on, tap with three fingers. If you hear the TalkBack menu, they work. If you do not, open the menu with a one-finger swipe down then right, or swipe up then right.

Moving around and activating

  • Move to the next item: swipe right with one finger. VoiceOver: also swipe right.
  • Move to the previous item: swipe left with one finger. VoiceOver: also swipe left.
  • Explore by touch: drag one finger around the screen. VoiceOver: the same.
  • Select an item (hear it, without opening it): tap once. VoiceOver: the same.
  • Activate the focused item: double-tap. VoiceOver: the same.
  • Long-press the focused item (the equivalent of a “touch and hold”): double-tap and hold. VoiceOver uses a triple-tap for touch-and-hold, so this is a difference.
  • Split-tap, added in TalkBack 16.2: move one finger to an item, and without lifting it, tap the screen with a second finger to activate it. VoiceOver has the same idea: touch and hold with one finger, then tap with another. Official source: What’s new with TalkBack 16.2.

Scrolling and reading

  • Scroll up, down, left or right: swipe with two fingers in that direction. This is a common trip-up. VoiceOver scrolls a page with a three-finger swipe, and the swipe direction is reversed from the page movement (a three-finger swipe up moves the content up, which shows the next page down). TalkBack’s two-finger swipe is the everyday scroll, in the direction you want the content to move.
  • Pause or resume speech: two-finger tap. VoiceOver: also two-finger tap.
  • Read from the next item: two-finger triple-tap (multi-finger gesture). VoiceOver uses a two-finger swipe down to read from the selected item, and a two-finger swipe up to read from the top. TalkBack’s “read from top / read from focused item” also lives in the TalkBack menu.
  • Start or stop media, and answer or hang up a call: two-finger double-tap. VoiceOver’s Magic Tap is also a two-finger double-tap, and does the same kind of job.

The TalkBack menu, and VoiceOver’s rotor

  • Open the TalkBack menu: three-finger tap, or a one-finger swipe down then right. This is where you will find Read from focused item, Copy last spoken phrase, Screen search, Hide or show screen, Voice commands, TalkBack settings, and, on current versions, image and screen descriptions.
  • VoiceOver has no exact menu like this. Many of those actions sit in the rotor, in the Item Chooser (two-finger triple-tap), or in VoiceOver settings.
  • TalkBack’s closest equivalent to the VoiceOver rotor is reading controls, described in the next section.

System gestures: Home, Back, Recents, notifications

Android has no Home button on a modern Pixel. It uses either gesture navigation (the default) or three-button navigation. With TalkBack and gesture navigation, official help says you use two fingers for the system gestures that a sighted person does with one finger.

  • Home: one-finger swipe up then left, or, with gesture navigation, a two-finger swipe up from the bottom.
  • Back: one-finger swipe down then left, or, with gesture navigation, a two-finger swipe from the left or right edge.
  • Recents (the app switcher): one-finger swipe left then up, or, with gesture navigation, a two-finger swipe up from the bottom and hold.
  • Show notifications: one-finger swipe right then down, or a two-finger swipe down from the top.
  • Show Quick Settings: while notifications are open, two-finger swipe down again.
  • Open the app list from the Home screen: two-finger swipe up.

VoiceOver comparison, from Apple’s VoiceOver gestures guide:

  • On iPhone models with no Home button, you go Home by sliding one finger up from the bottom until you feel a vibration, then lifting. TalkBack, by contrast, wants two fingers from the bottom, or the up-then-left L-shaped swipe.
  • VoiceOver uses a two-finger scrub (two fingers zig-zag in a “z”) to dismiss an alert or go back. TalkBack’s Back is down then left, or a two-finger edge swipe.
  • VoiceOver’s Control Centre and Notification Centre start at the status bar or the top edge. TalkBack’s notification shade is right-then-down, or two fingers from the top.

Other TalkBack gestures worth learning early

  • Screen search (find text on the current screen): swipe left then down.
  • Practise gestures: four-finger tap.
  • TalkBack help: four-finger double-tap.
  • Pass-through (the next gesture is treated as if TalkBack were off): four-finger double-tap and hold, or three-finger double-tap, according to the official gesture list. If you are unsure which your phone uses, check TalkBack settings, Gestures, rather than guessing.
  • Move a slider, such as volume, once it has focus: swipe up, or right then left, to increase; swipe down, or left then right, to decrease.
  • Text selection (multi-finger): two-finger double-tap and hold to start or end selection mode; three-finger double-tap to copy; three-finger double-tap and hold to cut; three-finger triple-tap to paste.

Most TalkBack gestures can be reassigned in TalkBack settings, Gestures. Two-finger swipe gestures cannot be reassigned. If a default gesture is awkward with one hand, it is worth customising. Official source: Use TalkBack gestures.

Reading controls

Reading controls are TalkBack’s rotor. They change what a swipe up or down will do: move by character, word, line or paragraph; jump by headings, links or controls; change speech rate; search the screen; and, in a table, move by row or column.

To cycle the reading control:

  • On a Pixel with multi-finger gestures: three-finger swipe up or down. You can also use a one-finger swipe up then down, or down then up.
  • On older one-finger-only devices: swipe up then down, or down then up.

Once a control is chosen, swipe down to go to the next item of that type, or swipe up to go to the previous one. For speech rate, swipe up to speak faster and swipe down to speak slower.

Default reading controls, from Use the TalkBack menu and reading controls:

  • Characters, words, lines and paragraphs.
  • Rows and columns, for tables. TalkBack 16.0 can switch to table navigation automatically when you enter a table. You can turn that on or off in TalkBack settings, Advanced settings.
  • Headings, controls (buttons, tick boxes, text fields) and links.
  • Speech rate and spoken language (if you have more than one text-to-speech language installed).
  • Screen search.

You can add more controls, such as buttons, tick boxes, edit fields, heading levels 1 to 6, images and videos, lists, list items, tables, combo boxes and visited links. Path: TalkBack settings, Edit menus or Customise menus, Customise reading controls.

The TalkBack menu itself, opened with a three-finger tap, includes Read from focused item, Copy last spoken phrase, Spoken language, Screen search, Hide or show screen, Voice commands, and settings for TalkBack, text-to-speech and braille. Options also change with context: Actions, Edit options, Links, and Add or edit label when those apply.

TalkBack 16.0 and later, in English, also add Describe image, Describe screen, and Ask Gemini to the menu, so you can hear a generated description of an image or of the current screen, then ask a follow-up question. Official source: What’s new with TalkBack 16.0.

TalkBack 16.2 can announce text formatting changes (bold, italics, underline, font size, colour). Turn this on in TalkBack settings, Verbosity, Speak text formatting, or add it to reading controls.

TalkBack voice commands

From TalkBack 9.1 onwards you can speak many TalkBack actions. Official help: Use TalkBack voice commands.

  • Start listening: swipe right then up with one finger, wait for the confirmation sound, then speak. You can change this gesture in TalkBack settings, Customise gestures.
  • Useful commands include: “Read from next”, “Read from top”, “Next heading”, “Next link”, “Next control”, “Talk faster”, “Talk slower”, “Find” followed by a word, “Type” or “Dictate” followed by text, “Copy”, “Paste”, “Home”, “Back”, “Notifications”, “Quick Settings”, “Recent apps”, “Hide screen”, “Show screen”, and “TalkBack settings”.
  • To cancel, say “Stop”, “Quit”, “Quiet”, “Pause” or “Never mind”.

This is not the same as Voice Access. TalkBack voice commands control the screen reader. Voice Access, covered later, controls the whole phone.

TalkBack braille keyboard

The TalkBack braille keyboard lets you type six-dot braille with six fingers on the screen. TalkBack must be on, and magnification must be off. Official help: Use the TalkBack braille keyboard.

To set it up: open the TalkBack menu, then TalkBack settings, Braille keyboard, Set up braille keyboard, and follow the spoken steps. In an app such as Gmail or Keep, focus a text field, double-tap, choose Switch input method or Next language, and select TalkBack braille keyboard. A tutorial is offered the first time.

There are two typing postures:

  • Screen-away mode: the screen faces away from you, in landscape. Your left hand holds the top of the phone, your right hand holds the bottom (USB port end). This is for typing with the phone held in front of you, screen outward.
  • Tabletop mode: the phone lies on a flat surface with the charging port on the left. Dots from left to right are 3, 2, 1, 4, 5 and 6, like a Perkins Brailler. The phone should announce “Tabletop mode” when it detects a flat surface.

By default the keyboard switches mode with the phone’s orientation. You can lock the layout in TalkBack settings, Braille keyboard, Layout. If the dots feel wrongly placed, hold all six dots for about three seconds until you hear that the positions have been updated, or use Update dot positions from the braille keyboard menu (three-finger swipe up).

While the braille keyboard is open, ordinary TalkBack gestures are not available. Official basic gestures:

  • Space: swipe right.
  • Delete a letter: swipe left.
  • Delete a word: two-finger swipe left.
  • New line: two-finger swipe right.
  • Submit: two-finger swipe up.
  • Hide the keyboard and return to ordinary TalkBack gestures: two-finger swipe down.
  • Switch to the next keyboard: three-finger swipe down.
  • Help and other options, including language and grade 1 or grade 2 braille: three-finger swipe up.

There is a longer official list for cursor movement, selection, copy, cut, paste and proofreading, including hold-a-dot-then-swipe gestures. See the official page rather than memorising them all at once. You can also connect a refreshable braille display; TalkBack 16.2 adds browse-mode shortcuts on the display and a way to read system messages such as notifications. Official path for the display shortcut list: Settings, Accessibility, TalkBack, Settings, Braille display.

A known official quirk: on some devices, if you close the braille keyboard in the Messages app, the Back button may not work. The official workaround is to go to the Home screen and continue from there.

Bluetooth and external keyboard shortcuts

A Bluetooth keyboard is often easier than on-screen typing, especially if you already think in keyboard commands. Connect the keyboard in Android Settings, Connected devices. Official TalkBack help: Use TalkBack keyboard shortcuts.

TalkBack has two keymaps:

  • Default keymap: fewer shortcuts. The TalkBack key is Alt or the Action key.
  • Enhanced keymap: more shortcuts, designed for web browsing, with Browse mode. On first activation of TalkBack 16.2 or later, Enhanced keymap is on by default.

The Action key is the Windows key on a Windows keyboard, or Command on a Mac keyboard. In Enhanced keymap, the TalkBack key is this Action key. You can change the modifier in TalkBack settings, Advanced settings, Keyboard shortcuts, Choose modifier key. TalkBack 16.2 includes a keyboard tutorial: TalkBack menu, TalkBack settings, TalkBack tutorial, when a physical keyboard is connected.

Browse mode, on the Enhanced keymap, is turned on by double-pressing the TalkBack key. In Browse mode you can jump by headings, links, lists and tables with single letter keys, similar in spirit to NVDA or JAWS. Smart browse mode (TalkBack 16.2) turns Browse mode off automatically when you enter an editable field so that you can type, then turns it back on when you leave the field.

Shortcuts that official help clearly labels, and that are safe to learn first (TalkBack key is Alt or Action, depending on keymap):

  • Next item: TalkBack key + Right arrow.
  • Previous item: TalkBack key + Left arrow.
  • Next or previous item by the current reading control: TalkBack key + Up arrow or Down arrow.
  • First item: TalkBack key + Ctrl + Left arrow. Last item: TalkBack key + Ctrl + Right arrow.
  • Back: TalkBack key + Delete.
  • Notifications: TalkBack key + Ctrl + N.
  • Play or pause media: TalkBack key + Shift + Space.
  • Open the apps list: double-press the Action key.
  • Turn TalkBack on or off (Android 16 and later): Action + Alt + T.
  • Visited links (TalkBack 16.0 and later): TalkBack key + V, and TalkBack key + Shift + V for the previous visited link.

There is a much longer official list for Chrome web navigation, landmarks, headings 1 to 6, and informational commands such as speaking the time or battery. Some labels did not come through clearly when the official tables were retrieved for this guide, so those extra shortcuts are not listed here. Use the in-app keyboard tutorial and the official shortcut page, and reassign anything that feels wrong.

Other useful keyboard shortcuts that are not TalkBack-specific:

  • Ctrl + Alt + Z triggers the same accessibility shortcut as holding both volume keys, once that volume-key shortcut is enabled.
  • On Android 16, Action + Alt + T toggles TalkBack. On Android 17 and later, Action + Alt + S toggles Select to Speak, according to official Select to Speak help. Whether a given Pixel 10 or 11 ships with Android 16 or 17 should be checked on the phone itself in Settings, About phone.

Select to Speak

Select to Speak is a “read this bit aloud” tool. It is not a full screen reader. People often use it alongside TalkBack, or instead of TalkBack when they only want a paragraph read. Official help: Use Select to Speak.

  • Turn it on in Settings, Accessibility, Select to Speak, and enable its shortcut. If you cannot find it, install or update Android Accessibility Suite from the Play Store.
  • The usual shortcut is a two-finger swipe up, or a three-finger swipe up if TalkBack is on, or the Accessibility button.
  • Then tap an item to hear it, drag a finger across several items, or activate Play to hear everything. You can change speed, pause, and skip in the player.
  • You can also select text in a supporting app and choose More, then Read aloud, without turning Select to Speak on.
  • There is an option to let it keep reading in the background while you move to another app.
  • You can point the Camera app at printed text or a picture and use Select to Speak to hear it. Official language list for this camera method includes English and many European languages.

Official help notes that Select to Speak might not work with every mobile web browser. Chrome is the safe first choice.

Voice Access, for fully hands-free control

Voice Access is the Android feature that lets you control the phone entirely by speaking: open apps, tap buttons, scroll, type, and move around the system. Official help: Use Voice Access commands. This is likely to matter to you more than to many people, because you already prefer voice, and because you want games and other apps to work when your hands are not free.

It supports English, Spanish, German, Italian, French, Portuguese and Japanese. Official help says Portuguese and Japanese only have the top 25 commands.

Turning it on and starting it

  • Install Voice Access if it is not already on the phone, then turn it on in Settings, Accessibility, Voice Access.
  • Start it by saying “Hey Google, Voice Access” or, if Gemini is your assistant, “Hey Google, start Voice Access”. Official pages use both wordings. One official note says the “start Voice Access” wording works with Gemini and not with the older Assistant. If one phrase fails, try the other.
  • You can also start it from a notification, from the Voice Access app, from an on-screen activation button, or from a physical key or switch configured in Voice Access settings.

Everyday commands

  • Navigation: “Open Gmail”, “Go home”, “Go back”, “Show notifications”, “Show Quick Settings”, “Show recent apps”, “Show previous app”, “Lock screen”, “Take screenshot”.
  • On a screen: “Tap compose”, or “Tap” followed by the name of any visible text or label. If several items match, Voice Access asks “Which one?” and you say the number.
  • “Show numbers” puts a number on every tappable item until you say “Hide numbers”. “Show labels” does the same with names, and hides automatically when the screen changes.
  • Help: “What can I say?”, “Show all commands”, “Open tutorial”, “Stop Voice Access”.
  • Typing and editing: “Type” or dictate into a field, then “Replace X with Y”, “Capitalise X”, “Delete X”, “Go to the beginning”, “Select X”, “Copy”, “Paste”, “New line”.
  • After one command, Voice Access listens again for the next, which is what makes truly hands-free chains possible: “Open Messages”, “Tap compose”, then dictate, then “Tap send”.

Voice Access can run at the same time as TalkBack, but two listening systems plus Gemini can talk over each other. A practical pattern used by many people is: TalkBack on for exploration, Voice Access started when you want a stretch of hands-free control, and “Stop Voice Access” when you are done. This combination has not been tested for this guide on a Pixel, so treat that as advice to try, not as a proven setup.

Voice Access needs a reasonably clear spoken command. If your voice varies, “Show numbers” and then “Tap 3” is often more reliable than hoping it hears a long button name.

Accessibility shortcuts

Android lets you assign a shortcut to TalkBack, Select to Speak, Voice Access, Magnification, Live Transcribe, Guided vision and other tools. Official help: Use accessibility shortcuts.

Path: Settings, Accessibility, then the feature, then its shortcut setting. Choices typically include:

  • The Accessibility button, either in the navigation bar (three-button navigation) or as a floating button.
  • Hold both volume keys.
  • A finger swipe: two-finger swipe up from the bottom, or three-finger swipe if TalkBack is on.
  • A Quick Settings tile.
  • Triple-tap for Magnification.

If more than one feature shares a shortcut, hold the shortcut, then choose from a menu. The volume-key shortcut can also be triggered from a keyboard with Ctrl + Alt + Z. You can later turn the volume-key shortcut off if you keep hitting it by accident in a pocket or on a bus.

If you prefer the old-style Back, Home and Recents buttons, you can switch from gesture navigation to three-button navigation in Settings, System, Gestures, System navigation. Some people coming from an iPhone with a Home button find three-button navigation easier at first. Others prefer gestures. Either works with TalkBack.

Pixel-specific features

A Pixel is usually the smoothest Android phone for TalkBack, because Google builds TalkBack and the Pixel software together. Features below are documented for recent Pixels. Where a feature is limited to a certain model or country, that is stated.

  • Automatic image descriptions, powered by on-device generative AI, are available on Pixel 9 and later, which includes Pixel 10 and 11. When TalkBack focus lands on an image in a supported app, a description can be generated automatically. You can choose All images, Unlabelled images only, or Don’t describe images, in TalkBack settings under Automatic descriptions. Official help: Use image descriptions in TalkBack.
  • Describe image, Describe screen and Ask Gemini (English) are in the TalkBack menu on current TalkBack versions, as described earlier.
  • Gemini is the default assistant on Pixel 9 and later, including Pixel 10 and 11. “Hey Google” and a press-and-hold of the power button open Gemini. Official help: Use Gemini on your Pixel phone.
  • Guided vision in Gemini Live can describe what the camera sees, help you reframe the shot, read labels and identify objects. Official help says it is rolling out slowly, so it may not be on the phone yet. It is not a mobility or navigation aid. You can start it from the TalkBack menu (Guided vision) or assign it as an accessibility shortcut. Official help: Get audio descriptions with guided vision in Gemini Live.
  • Lookout, Live Transcribe, Recorder with English (United Kingdom) transcription, Call Screen, and Live Caption are all first-class on a Pixel. Each has its own section below.
  • Call Screen automatic screening is officially available in the UK. Some newer Call Screen behaviours on Pixel 11 are officially limited to a US SIM and English in the United States. See Phone and Call Screen.
  • Gboard’s advanced voice typing is officially for Pixel 6 and later. Some editing features are US-only. See Gboard voice typing.
  • Hearing aid support, Sound Amplifier, Live Caption, and Audio description (for videos that provide it, on Android 13 and later) are in Settings, Accessibility.

If you are choosing between Pixel 10 and Pixel 11, official help already names both. This guide cannot honestly rank them for TalkBack quality, because that has not been tested here. For accessibility, the software features above matter more than a modest hardware bump, except where a feature is listed as “Pixel 11 and later”. Check the UK feature list on the official Pixel pages before you buy.

How web vibration works in Android Chrome

This matters for the web apps you already use, such as games and meditation pages that try to vibrate the phone.

  • Chrome for Android supports the web Vibration API, navigator.vibrate(). MDN and Can I use both list it as supported in Chrome for Android. Official MDN page: Navigator.vibrate().
  • A page can only vibrate after a “sticky user activation”. In practice, you need to have tapped or double-tapped something on that page first. A vibration that starts all by itself, with no tap, is usually blocked.
  • Vibration can also fail if the phone is in Silent or Do Not Disturb mode, or if vibration is turned off in system settings. MDN mentions this.
  • TalkBack has its own vibration feedback setting, in TalkBack settings. That setting is for TalkBack’s own ticks and cues. It does not replace the web Vibration API, and it does not remove the need for a tap on the page.
  • Safari on iPhone does not support the Vibration API. Can I use lists Safari on iOS as not supported, including current versions retrieved in November 2026. Chrome on iPhone uses Apple’s WebKit, so it is in the same position: web vibration should not be expected on the iPhone at all.

Practical advice for your apps: on a Pixel, open the page in Chrome (not a tiny in-app browser), double-tap once to start or to focus the page, then use the app as usual. If you feel no vibration, check that vibration is on, that Do Not Disturb is off, and that you have tapped the page after it loaded. This behaviour has been verified from documentation only, not by running your apps on a Pixel or an iPhone for this report.

What it is for

On a Pixel with TalkBack

The Docs Android app can read and edit, but the web editor in Chrome is the more complete screen-reader experience, especially for headings, comments and long documents. On iPhone with VoiceOver, and on the web

The iOS app is fine for short edits. For serious writing, open the document at docs.google.com or create a new one at docs.new.

Essential official steps on the web:

  • Turn on screen reader support if you do not hear “Screen reader support enabled” when the document opens. Shortcut: Command + Option + Z on a Mac, Ctrl + Alt + Z on Windows. Or open the Tools menu (Control + Option + T on a Mac, Alt + Shift + T on Windows or ChromeOS) and tick Turn on screen reader support.

  • Open the shortcut list with Command + / or Ctrl + /. Search the menus with Option + / or Alt + /.
  • Move between the menu bar, side panel and main landmarks with Command + Option + full stop (or comma to go the other way). On Windows the equivalent is Ctrl + Alt + full stop or comma.
  • Context menu: Shift + F10.
  • Voice typing on the web (computer): Ctrl + Shift + S, according to the official Workspace shortcut list. Availability depends on the browser and language.

Use headings (Heading 1, Heading 2, and so on) rather than large bold text. Screen readers, including TalkBack and VoiceOver, can then jump by heading. In Docs you apply these from the styles menu or with the official heading shortcuts listed in the in-app shortcut help.

  • The mobile apps hide a lot of structure. If navigation by heading fails, open the document on the web.
  • Comments and suggestion mode can be announced late or out of order. There are official “verbalise comment” shortcuts once screen reader support is on (on Windows, hold Ctrl + Alt, then A then C, according to the Workspace list).
  • Other people’s cursors can cause a stream of “so-and-so is editing” messages. Official help explains how to turn collaborator announcements off.
  • If focus jumps out of the document, press Escape, which official help says returns focus to the text.

Lookout

What it is for

Lookout is a Google Android app for people who are blind or have low vision. It uses the camera to read text, recognise food labels and some banknotes, describe images, and help you find certain objects. Official help: Use Lookout to explore your surroundings. It is not pre-described as an iPhone app; on iPhone, the closest built-in tools are VoiceOver Recognition, the Magnifier, and Lookout is not the official path. After you move to a Pixel, Lookout becomes one of your main everyday tools.

On a Pixel with TalkBack

Install “Lookout – assisted vision” from the Play Store, or say “Hey Google, start Lookout”. Allow the camera. Hold the phone so the camera faces outwards. Official help lists seven modes, switched from Select mode at the top left (double-tap with TalkBack):

  • Text: point at text to hear it read. Rotate between portrait and landscape, or move closer or further, if reading is unclear. You can set language to Detect language.
  • Explore (beta): hears objects and text as you pan. Official help says this mode is less accurate than the others.
  • Food labels: scan a barcode or the front of a food pack. Officially available only in some countries; the first time, it may download extra data and then work offline. You can set the country in the mode.
  • Documents: read a full page, with spoken guidance to line the page up. There is a Take snapshot button, which official help says can sometimes read better than the live view. Reading tools can change speed, voice, font and contrast.
  • Currency: reads one banknote at a time. Officially only US dollars, euros or Indian rupees, and not coins. This is important in the UK: British pounds are not on that official list, so do not rely on Lookout to identify UK notes.
  • Images: capture, upload or share a picture and hear a description, including text and objects. Detailed descriptions are available in English. In the US, UK and Canada, if you use English, you can ask follow-up questions by voice or keyboard. Lighting, blur and a busy picture can all reduce accuracy.
  • Find: choose from a list (default is seating and tables; also doors and windows, cups, bottles and cans, utensils and tableware, vehicles, and bathroom). Pan slowly. Official help says most phones can give direction and distance to the object.

Other official tips:

  • The camera on/off control vibrates when you turn the camera on, if haptic feedback is enabled in Lookout settings.
  • Recent results can be reviewed and shared, but they are deleted when you close the app.
  • You can set a torch to come on automatically, change Lookout’s own text-to-speech (separate from the system voice), and turn off Images mode data collection in Lookout settings if you prefer.

On iPhone with VoiceOver, for now

Lookout is an Android app. Until you have a Pixel, use iPhone tools for the same jobs: VoiceOver Recognition, Magnifier, the Camera app with VoiceOver, and, for some document reading, a third-party app you already trust. This guide does not recommend a paid replacement; Lookout itself will cover a lot of this once you move.

Pain points and workarounds

  • Do not use Lookout, or Gemini guided vision, as a substitute for a cane, a guide, or safe crossing practice. Official guided-vision help makes a similar warning, and it applies here too.
  • UK banknotes are not in the official Currency list. Use another method for cash.
  • Food labels and some other modes depend on country data. If a mode asks to download data, do that on Wi-Fi.
  • Explore mode is officially beta and less accurate. Prefer Text, Documents, Images or Find when you need a reliable answer.

Google Maps

What it is for

Maps is for search, walking, public transport and exploring what is around you. For you, the important pieces are walking navigation with detailed voice guidance, and hearing nearby places through Lens in Maps. Official Android accessibility page: Accessibility in Google Maps (Android). The original detailed voice guidance announcement is in Voice guidance in Maps, built for people with impaired vision. On a Pixel with TalkBack

Detailed voice guidance for walking

This is the feature built for people with visual impairments. Official and later Google descriptions say it tells you that you are on the correct route, the distance and direction to the next turn, when you are approaching a large intersection, and when you are being rerouted. To turn it on, official and widely repeated Google instructions are:

  • Open Google Maps.
  • Open your profile picture, then Settings, then Navigation.
  • Under Walking options, turn on Detailed voice guidance.

Do this at home, before you need it on a bus journey or an unfamiliar road. Keep spoken volume high, and consider an earpiece that does not block environmental sound. Detailed guidance is a support, not a replacement, for a cane, a guide, or listening to the street.

Lens in Maps, for “what is around me”

Official Android help, not Look Around, is the documented screen-reader way to hear nearby places:

  • In the Maps search bar, activate Lens.
  • Hold the phone up and point the camera at buildings, shops or signs.
  • Pan slowly. TalkBack should speak the name and category of places, and how far away they are, so you can orient yourself.
  • Double-tap a place for more details. Category chips such as restaurants or café can narrow the search.
  • Official limits: TalkBack must be on, and Lens in Maps is not supported on tablet-sized devices.

Look Around (the immersive street-level view) is a different feature. This guide could not verify solid, official TalkBack support for Look Around itself. Until that is documented clearly, use Lens in Maps and walking guidance instead, and treat Look Around as unproven for your use. See What could not be verified.

Public transport and accessible places

For a wheelchair-accessible transit route: enter the destination, choose the place, choose Directions and Public transport, then Options, and under Routes turn on Wheelchair-accessible. For places, official Android help says you can turn on Emphasise accessibility info in Settings, App and display, then look for wheelchair information on a place’s About tab. That information is mainly about wheelchair access (entrance, toilets, seating, parking, lift). It is still useful background, but it is not a vision-specific rating.

On iPhone with VoiceOver, and on the web

The Maps iOS app works with VoiceOver, and Google’s 2024 announcement said detailed voice guidance and Lens-in-Maps screen-reader support were expanded to Android and iOS in supported languages. Use the same Settings, Navigation, Walking options path on iPhone. The web app at maps.google.com has keyboard navigation (Tab to the map, arrows to move, plus and minus to zoom, Ctrl + / for shortcuts) but it is a poor substitute for spoken walking guidance on a phone.

Pain points and workarounds

  • The map canvas itself is visual. Do not try to “read the map”. Use search, the results list, the directions list, and spoken guidance.
  • GPS can be wrong between tall buildings or after a bus ride. If the voice says you have left the route and that does not match what you feel, stop and check, rather than following a bad reroute.
  • With TalkBack, map gestures change. Official help describes one-finger drag and double-tap-then-drag to zoom for sighted or low-vision use; with TalkBack you will more often rely on buttons, search and the directions sheet. Two-finger gestures are reserved for scrolling and system actions, so casual map dragging is easy to get wrong. Prefer search and the list.
  • Offline maps can help when the signal drops, but detailed guidance still depends on location. Download an offline area for your part of London or the UK before you travel.

Google Chrome

What it is for

Chrome is Google’s web browser, and the best browser to pair with TalkBack and with Google’s web apps. Official help: Use TalkBack to browse the web with Chrome. Many of your own web apps will be opened here.

On a Pixel with TalkBack

What it is for

The Phone app places and receives calls. On a Pixel, Call Screen can answer first, ask who is calling and why, and show you a live transcript before you pick up. Official help: Screen your calls before you answer them. Official help says Call Screen works on the device and does not use Wi-Fi or mobile data, and that automatic screening is available in the UK (and in Australia, Canada, Ireland and the US). Manual screening is available in those countries and in several others. A UK SIM is required for the UK feature.

On a Pixel with TalkBack

  • If you cannot find Screen call, official help says to open the Call Screen settings and follow any extra on-screen steps; if there are no Call Screen settings at all, the device does not support it.
  • Do not rely on Call Screen to catch every scam call.

Glossary

  • Accessibility shortcut. A button, a volume-key hold, or a swipe that turns an accessibility tool on or off, or switches between tools.
  • Action key. On an external keyboard used with Android, the Windows key or, on a Mac keyboard, the Command key. TalkBack uses it in the Enhanced keymap and in shortcuts such as Action + Alt + T.
  • Browse mode. A TalkBack Enhanced keymap mode in which letter keys jump around a web page, similar in spirit to a desktop screen reader. Double-press the TalkBack key to turn it on or off.
  • Call Screen. A Pixel feature that answers a call first, asks who is calling, and shows you a transcript before you pick up.
  • Gboard. Google’s on-screen keyboard, including voice typing.
  • Gemini. Google’s current AI assistant, and the default on Pixel 9 and later.
  • Google Assistant. The older assistant. Some phrases and devices still use this name.
  • Guided vision. A Gemini Live feature that describes what the camera sees and helps you line the camera up. Officially not for navigation.
  • Lens in Maps. A camera mode inside Google Maps that can speak nearby places when TalkBack is on.
  • Look Around. A visual, street-level view in Maps. Not the same as Lens in Maps, and not verified in this guide as a TalkBack tool.
  • Lookout. A Google Android app that reads text, describes images and helps you find certain objects with the camera.
  • Passkey. A way to sign in with your phone’s lock (PIN, fingerprint or face) instead of typing a password. It stays on your devices.
  • RCS. A richer kind of text message used by Google Messages, now also able to work between many Android phones and iPhones.
  • Reading controls. TalkBack’s equivalent of the VoiceOver rotor: a setting that changes what a swipe up or down will do, such as move by heading or change speech rate.
  • Rotor. VoiceOver’s two-finger rotate control, which chooses whether a swipe up or down moves by heading, word, character, and so on.
  • Screen-away mode. A TalkBack braille keyboard posture in which the phone’s screen faces away from you while you type braille.
  • Select to Speak. An Android tool that reads a selected part of the screen aloud, without being a full screen reader.
  • Split-tap. Touch an item with one finger and, without lifting, tap with a second finger to activate it. Available in both VoiceOver and recent TalkBack.
  • TalkBack. The screen reader built into Android.
  • TalkBack key. The modifier key for TalkBack keyboard shortcuts, usually Alt or the Action key, depending on the keymap.
  • TalkBack menu. A menu of TalkBack actions, opened with a three-finger tap or a swipe down then right.
  • Vibration API. A web feature that lets a page vibrate the phone. It works in Chrome on Android after a tap on the page. It does not work in Safari on iPhone.
  • Voice Access. An Android tool that controls the phone by spoken commands, such as “Open Gmail” or “Tap compose”.
  • VoiceOver. The screen reader built into iPhone, iPad and Mac.
  • 2-Step Verification (2SV). An extra check, after your password, when you sign in to your Google Account. A passkey can take the place of this pair of steps.

What could not be verified

These points were researched carefully, but they could not be confirmed as firmly as the rest. They are listed so that you can treat them with caution.

  • This entire guide was not tested on an iPhone 11 with VoiceOver, and not tested on a Pixel 10 or Pixel 11 with TalkBack. Gestures and menu paths come from official help current in late November 2026, not from a device under the author’s fingers.
  • Pixel 11, Pixel 11 Pro, Pixel 11 Pro XL and Pixel 11 Pro Fold are named in official Google help (for example Call Screen and Live Transcribe). UK shop availability, price, and every software difference versus Pixel 10 were not independently checked.
  • The exact Android version on a UK Pixel 10 or 11 (16, 17, or another) was not confirmed. Official pages already mention Android 16 (TalkBack keyboard toggle) and Android 17 (Select to Speak keyboard toggle). Check Settings, About phone, on the device you buy.
  • Some official TalkBack keyboard shortcut tables did not yield clear labels for every key in the text that could be retrieved. Only clearly labelled shortcuts are listed in this guide. Use the official shortcut page and the in-app keyboard tutorial for the rest.
  • Look Around in Google Maps was not found, in official accessibility help, as a fully documented TalkBack orientation tool. Lens in Maps is the officially documented method. Whether Look Around is usable with TalkBack remains uncertain.
  • The precise Settings path for detailed voice guidance was taken from Google’s original announcement and later reputable write-ups (profile, Settings, Navigation, Walking options). The main Android Maps accessibility page retrieved for this guide described Lens, transit and wheelchair information, but did not repeat that walking path in the same document. If the menu names differ on your app version, search Settings for “Detailed voice guidance” or “Walking”.
  • Gboard “Fix it” and detailed voice edits are officially US-only at the time of the official help retrieved. They may or may not appear on a UK Pixel. Writing-tools voice commands on Pixel 9 (excluding 9a) and later were not tested in the UK.
  • The two official wordings “Hey Google, Voice Access” and “Hey Google, start Voice Access” both appear in Google help, with a note that one form works with Gemini rather than Assistant. Which wording your Pixel accepts should be tried on the device.
  • Web vibration in Chrome on Android, and its absence in Safari on iOS, is documented by MDN and Can I use. It was not tested with your own web apps on a Pixel or an iPhone for this report. Silent mode, Do Not Disturb, and a missing first tap are common reasons for it to fail even on Android.
  • No dedicated official “Use Google Tasks with a screen reader” page was found. Tasks guidance in this guide is therefore general.
  • Official Wallet accessibility help does not spell out every TalkBack step at a UK contactless terminal. First payments should be treated as a practice run.
  • Official Google Fit accessibility help is partly dated (it still mentions old TalkBack context menus). Watch-face accessibility for Fitbit or Pixel Watch was not fully verified from the pages retrieved.
  • Independent older reviews of TalkBack issues in YouTube Music were not re-tested against the late-2026 app.
  • How TalkBack and Live Transcribe behave together on a rolling caption stream, and how the two-finger double-tap is handled inside Recorder, were not device-tested.
  • iMessage-specific features will not come with you to Android. The everyday experience of RCS between a UK Pixel and contacts on iPhone can vary with carrier support, and was not tested here.
  • Gemini Guided vision is officially rolling out slowly and may be absent on a given phone or in a given country at first.
